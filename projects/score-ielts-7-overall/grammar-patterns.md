# Grammar Patterns

The standing rules behind the repeat categories in [[projects/score-ielts-7-overall/error-log|the error log]] — nouns, articles, and everything else. Checked during the self-scoring pass, sentence by sentence, not while writing. Distilled from the 08-27 through 09-06 self-scores.

## Countable and Uncountable Nouns

### Uncountable Nouns Never Take *-s*

Check a noun against this list before pluralising it. These are the ones that have come up or are likely to, given the essay topics.

- data
- transportation
- equipment
- furniture
- luggage
- traffic
- information
- advice
- news
- homework
- research
- evidence
- progress
- accommodation

*transportations* has already appeared in a real essay, *a data* appeared on 2026-08-29, and *traffic* recurs across the congestion topics — those three are the live risks. *Data* matters most: it is the opening noun of almost every Task 1.

**They take a singular verb.** *The equipment is expensive*, not *are*. *Traffic has worsened*, not *have*. *This research shows*, not *show*. Subject-verb agreement is already the longest run of entries in the error log, so a wrongly pluralised uncountable usually drags a wrong verb along with it and costs two errors in one sentence.

To count them, count the container, not the noun: *a piece of equipment*, *two items of luggage*, *a great deal of progress* — and the verb then follows the container: *two items of luggage were lost*.

### Collective Nouns Don't Take *-s* Either

*Staff* (like *police*, *faculty*, *crew*) is a collective/group noun — never *staffs*. Say *"the staff are trained"* or use *staff members* for individuals. Same pattern as above: looks like it should pluralise, doesn't.

Whether a singular countable noun needs an article is an article rule, not a countability rule — see Articles below.

## Articles

Distilled from the 08-31 self-score and the 09-01 police-budget rep.

### Singular Countable Nouns Need an Article

If the noun is singular and countable, it needs `a`, `an`, `the`, or a possessive/demonstrative in front of it — no exceptions.

- ❌ "a household performed average of 50 hours" → ✅ "a household performed **an** average of 50 hours"
- ❌ "I have chance to learn before" → ✅ "I have **a** chance to learn before"
- ❌ "Second option is to ban heavy duty transportations" → ✅ "**A** second option is to ban heavy-duty transportation"

Scan habit: after writing a sentence, check every singular noun for an article or possessive right before it.

**This "no exceptions" rule is singular-only.** Plural countable nouns can stand alone with zero article when generic — "**cars** are useful," "**students** prefer online learning" — the same zero-article option uncountable nouns have. A singular countable noun has no such option; it is the one case that always needs a determiner. See the generic-vs-specific rule below for when a plural or uncountable noun does need *the*.

### Superlatives Always Take *the*

*The most, the least, the highest, the fastest* — a superlative names the one extreme out of three or more, so it is always definite.

- ❌ "42%, highest among three" → ✅ "42%, **the** highest of the three"

Compare the three comparison forms, since Task 1 uses all of them: *high* (positive, no comparison) → *higher than* (comparative, 2 things, no article) → ***the* highest of/among** (superlative, 3+ things, always *the*).

This is independent of the rule below — the superlative itself always takes *the*, but whatever noun follows "of" (e.g. *the highest of **the** money spent* vs *the highest of **money** spent*) is decided separately, by whether that noun is generic or specific.

### *The* Is About Generic vs. Specific — Not Countable vs. Uncountable

The instinct to reach for countable/uncountable here is wrong. Both countable and uncountable nouns drop *the* when used generically, and both take *the* when pointing at one specific, already-identified thing.

- **Generic (no *the*), uncountable:** "the proportion of **money** spent on salaries" — money spent in general, not one named sum.
- **Generic (no *the*), countable:** "the number of **cars** on the road increased" / "the proportion of **students** who prefer online learning" — cars, students in general, not a named group.
- **Specific (needs *the*), either kind:** "There were 200 cars in the survey; the proportion of **the cars** that were red was 20%" — one exact, previously-named set.

Fix for "the proportion of the cars ran through this road": drop *the* (generic group, not a pre-identified one) and add the missing relative pronoun — **"the proportion of cars *that* ran through this road."**

### *The* vs. *This/These* vs. *That/Those*

*The* carries no sense of near or far — just "specific and already identified," for singular, plural, or uncountable alike: *the chart*, *the data*, *the cars*.

*This/these* point near — just introduced, or physically close: "The chart shows three categories. **These** categories differ across income groups."

*That/those* point far — mentioned a while back, or set in contrast to something closer: "By 2020 housing spending had risen; food spending stayed close to **those** 1990 levels."

*This/these/that/those* also work as determiners in front of a noun (like *a/an/the*, per the singular-countable-noun rule above) or stand alone as pronouns.

### Missing Complementizer "That"

Dropped 5 times across the two 2026-09-05 essays, the single most-repeated slip so far.

| Pattern | ❌ | ✅ | Rule |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Noun + defining clause | "a school of thought a written exam is not the best way" | "a school of thought **that** a written exam is not the best way" | The clause defines the noun (*namely that...*) rather than describing something else — needs *that*. |
| *is/was* + full clause | "one advantage is this format testing the knowledge" | "one advantage is **that** this format tests the knowledge" | When *be* is followed by a whole clause (subject + verb), not just a noun phrase, *that* is required. |

Scan habit: after *is/are/argue/believe* + a noun like *fact, argument, belief, advantage, disadvantage, school of thought*, check whether a full clause follows. If it does, `that` goes right before it.

### Verb + Preposition Collocations

*agree* takes *with* before a noun object, and the noun still needs *that* before its own defining clause: ❌ "I agree the argument written exams are not..." → ✅ "I agree **with** the argument **that** written exams are not..."
