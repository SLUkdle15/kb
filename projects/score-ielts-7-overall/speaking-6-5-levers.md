# Speaking 6.5 Levers

Speaking is scored on four equally weighted criteria: Fluency and Coherence, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range and Accuracy, and Pronunciation. Fourteen minutes, three parts, one examiner, no second chances — which is exactly why the work happens on a phone recorder beforehand.

## What Separates 6 from 7

- **Fluency and Coherence.** Band 6 is willing to speak at length but loses coherence through repetition, self-correction, and hesitation. Band 7 speaks at length without noticeable effort, and any hesitation is about finding *language*, not about finding something to say. The difference is mostly hesitation *type*, not hesitation *amount*.
- **Lexical Resource.** Band 6 makes meaning clear despite inappropriate word choices and paraphrases with mixed success. Band 7 uses vocabulary flexibly and paraphrases effectively when the exact word will not come.
- **Grammatical Range and Accuracy.** Band 6 makes frequent errors in complex structures. Band 7 produces **frequent error-free sentences**. Same sentence as in [[projects/score-ielts-7-overall/writing-6-5-levers|Writing 6.5 Levers]], same consequence: clean beats ambitious.
- **Pronunciation.** Band 6 is generally understood but mispronunciation reduces clarity at times. Band 7 is easy to understand throughout, and the L1 accent has minimal effect. Accent is not penalised. Unclear sounds are.

## The Five Things Worth Fixing First

1. **Say the final consonants.** This is the highest-yield fix available and it scores twice. Vietnamese drops final stops and clusters, so *worked* comes out as *work*, *ideas* as *idea*, *last* as *las*. The examiner does not hear a pronunciation slip — they hear a missing past tense and a missing plural, and mark it under Grammatical Accuracy as well as Pronunciation. Drill `-s`, `-ed`, and final `-t/-k/-p/-st/-sk` deliberately, ten minutes on Thursdays, until they survive at speaking speed.

2. **Fill the full two minutes in Part 2, every time.** Running dry at fifty seconds is the single most common cause of a 5.5. Use the four-slot frame below and stop treating the cue card bullets as a checklist to sprint through.

3. **Answer Part 3 in four moves, not two.** Two-sentence answers read as band 5 no matter how correct the English. The move is: answer directly, give the reason, give a concrete example, then acknowledge the other side or the limit of the claim. Four moves takes about forty-five seconds, which is the right length.

4. **Hesitate in English, not in silence.** Fluency is scored on flow, not on precision. When the exact word will not come, paraphrase out loud — "the thing you use to..." — because effective paraphrase is explicitly a band 7 behaviour, while three seconds of silence hunting for the perfect noun is a band 5 one. Plain words said fluently outscore rare words said haltingly.

5. **Do not memorise answers.** Examiners are trained to hear rehearsed chunks, and memorised language is not evidence of range, so it earns nothing while making everything around it sound worse by contrast. Memorise *frames* — ways of structuring an answer — never sentences.

## The Part 2 Four-Slot Frame

One minute of preparation, four keywords on the paper, one per slot. Keywords, not sentences — writing sentences means reading them aloud, which kills fluency.

1. **Set the scene.** What, when, where. Two sentences, no more. Most people spend a minute here and then have nothing left.
2. **The detail the card asks for.** The bulk of the turn, roughly a minute. This is where description, comparison, and specifics go.
3. **What happened, or what you did.** Narrative pulls in past tenses naturally, which is free Grammatical Range that does not have to be forced.
4. **Why it mattered.** The last bullet on the card is almost always "and explain why...". It is also the slot that gets skipped when time runs out. Deliver it even if slot 2 has to be cut short — it is the part the card explicitly asked for.

If the two minutes end early, the frame was not used. If the examiner stops you mid-sentence, that is a good sign, not a bad one.

## Part 1 and Part 3 Lengths Are Different

- **Part 1** wants answer plus one reason or example — two or three sentences. One-word answers give the examiner nothing to score. Long speeches waste the warm-up and read as rehearsed.
- **Part 3** wants the four moves above. It is the abstract, opinion-driven part, and it is where the band is actually decided. Disagreeing with the examiner is allowed. So is "I have not thought about that before, but I would guess..." — that sentence buys thinking time in English and is itself a band 7 discourse behaviour.

## The Recording Loop

The speaking equivalent of the Wednesday rewrite. Never record a cue card once and move to a new one.

- [ ] Record the turn on the phone. Full two minutes, no restarts, no stopping to fix a sentence.
- [ ] Listen once for content only. Were all four slots covered? Was the "why" bullet actually answered?
- [ ] Listen again for fluency. Mark where the silences fell. Silence hunting for a word is fixable by paraphrasing; silence hunting for an idea is fixable by the frame.
- [ ] Transcribe sixty seconds of it. Painful, and the only way the real error pattern becomes visible.
- [ ] In the transcript, count sentences and count error-free sentences. Under half means simplify, not embellish.
- [ ] Listen for dropped final consonants specifically. Mark every `-s` and `-ed` that did not survive.
- [ ] Add repeated errors to [[projects/score-ielts-7-overall/error-log|Error Log]].
- [ ] **Record the same cue card again, immediately.** The second take is where the gain lives.

## On the Day

Arrive warm. Speak English for twenty minutes before the test — to a friend, to a recording, to nobody. A cold first Part 1 answer wastes the section where the examiner forms an impression.

Ask for a question to be repeated if it was not clear. That costs nothing and is better than answering a question that was not asked. Answering what you wished had been asked is a Fluency and Coherence penalty, and it is the trap that rehearsed topics set.
