## Three Families, One Toolkit Each

Every Task 1 prompt is one of three families, and each wants a different toolkit — the trend language below does not transfer to the other two, and using it anyway is the most likely new mistake.

1. **Chart/data** — line, bar, pie, table, and mixed combinations. Toolkit: everything below this section. Practiced repeatedly so far (household appliances, internet access, commuter transport, leisure activities).
2. **Process** — a natural or man-made sequence. Toolkit: present simple throughout, passive voice for what's done to the material, sequencing language (*first / once this is complete / following this / at the final stage*) instead of trend words, overview names stage count plus start/end (or notes a repeating cycle). First encountered 2026-09-01.
3. **Map** — two places, or the same place at two points in time. Toolkit: spatial/directional language (*north of, adjacent to, was replaced by*), contrast between the two points (past-vs-present or place-A-vs-place-B), overview names the single biggest change or difference. Not yet encountered in practice — a real coverage gap worth watching in [[projects/score-ielts-7-overall/cambridge-papers|Cambridge Papers Used]].

The rest of this note is the chart/data toolkit specifically.

## Reading a Table/Chart Before Writing

One minute of planning, before any sentence gets written. Same routine every time:

1. **Name the shape of the data.** What varies by row, what's compared by column.
2. **Compute the totals first** — that's the overview.
3. **Rank the categories, and check if the ranking changes** between the two points being compared.
4. **Find the biggest mover — in absolute terms AND as a share/percentage.** This is where the real feature hides: the biggest category and the fastest-growing category are often not the same one, and that contrast is worth a sentence.
5. **Check share-of-total, not just the raw number**, for a bonus feature if one shows up.
6. **Select 2–3 features that contrast with each other, not all the numbers.**

**Worked example — Paragraph 1, police budget sources (09-01 rep):** National Government / Local Taxes / Other, 2017 vs 2018.

- Rank: National Government stayed the largest source in both years — the ranking didn't change.
- Biggest mover: Local Taxes, both in absolute terms (+11.1m) and growth rate (+12.2%) — it drove most of the total's increase, while National Government (the biggest category) grew only +1.3%.
- Flat: Other sources moved only +0.5m, essentially unchanged.

Three features, one real contrast (biggest category ≠ fastest-growing category) — that's the paragraph, not a walk through all six numbers.

## Intro: Paraphrase, Then Overview

Two separate sentences, in this order:

1. **Paraphrase the question** — restate what the chart(s) show, in different words.
2. **Overview** — the one mandatory sentence naming the key trend(s), starting with a plain "Overall,". No overview caps Task Achievement at 5 regardless of anything else in the essay.

## Precise Collocations for Trends

Swap vague verbs for exact ones — a wrong collocation costs more in Lexical Resource than a plain word would have.

- Rising: *rose, climbed, increased steadily*
- Falling: *fell, declined, dropped sharply*
- **Never "subsided"** for a shrinking number — wrong register, already flagged once.

Add any newly corrected pair to [[projects/score-ielts-7-overall/repertoire|Repertoire]] so it gets reused, not just corrected once.

## State the Chart Relationship Explicitly

On a mixed-chart task, one sentence naming how the two charts relate is what separates a 6 from a 7:

> *"As ownership of household appliances rose, the time spent on housework fell correspondingly."*

Had all the data for this on 08-31 and never said it — check every mixed-chart task for a relationship sentence before submitting.

## Coherence: Kill the Mechanical Linkers

Replace "First,"/"Second," with referencing back to the previous sentence:

> *"This growth was mirrored by a steady decline in..."*
